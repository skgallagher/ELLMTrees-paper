With the sheer number of open models available, frequently fine-tuned from base models, along with the increasing cost of frontier models, it is critical to choose models that are fit for the user's goals. While users must consider how models perform on particular benchmarks most related to their primary goal, e.g. text summarization, users must also consider other criteria such as model strengths as a whole, failure modes, computation cost, interoperability, and safety \cite{}. While there are several methods and techniques to use for model selection \cite{} (benchmarks, cost, size), we study model lineage because it allows us to consider several model criteria at once. In particular, we examine how 1) model lineage can be recovered by examining changes in model parameters after fine-tuning; 2) how those changing model parameters are linked to useful model behavior; and 3) how that compares to lineage recovery based on model behavior alone.  

Consider a scenario where a company has a powerful base model that can be trained on their own data.  Imagine the model is then forked at different levels and fine-tuned on different data, one for finance, one for IT, one for fraud, etc.  Within those groups, those models may be further fine-tuned resulting in a complicated training lineage (see Fig. \ref{fig:overview}A). In a single report, dozens of versions of the same base model be used.  Since these models are used independently, it is possible that the exact training structure is unknown to the company.  For users, it is important to know which model or models should best be used to continue the analysis.  As such, s/he would want to know where the models diverge, where the mdoel strengths are, and perhaps most importantly, where the models will fail.  Thus it is important to  assess model changes first through changes i model parameters and subsequently link those changes in parameters to functional model behavior (Fig. \ref{fig:overview}B).  Finally, it is important to compare the company model to other available models, where the internal parameters are not available (see Fig. \ref{fig:overview}C) to ensure that the model is being used efficiently.

We apply lineage recovery estimators to fully recover model training lineage from model parameters alone.  To do this, we first develop a principled framework for estimating lineage through angles in weight-space.  We are able to determine which layers are strong lineage 'carriers.'  Then, with model lineage in hand, we then determine how changes in model parameters are linked to intended and unintentional behavior.  Finally, we compare the lineage recovery using model parameters to current methods that use lineage recovery based on model behavior alone.  We test our methods on a encoder-decoder model and decoder models of varying scale and fine-tuning strategies.  We find that both architecture and dimensionality are important indicators in strength of lineage estimation.

The rest of this paper proceeds as follows.  In \S \ref{sec:related} we examine how previous works approached these three lineage problems at parameter-only, parameter-behavior, and behavior-only levels.  In \S \ref{sec:framework}, we show how we determine model training signal through careful metric selection and summary statistics, guided by mathematical principles.  In \S \ref{sec:experiments}, we discuss how we turn our framework into testable experiments.  Following that in \S \ref{sec:results}, we show our results for lineage recovery and parameter-behavior association.  Finally, we discuss limitations and future work in \S \ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

LLM lineage has become an increasingly studied topic, appearing in three broad forms:
\emph{model-parameter} studies, \emph{weight--behavior} studies that align parameters with
outputs, and \emph{response-output} studies. Across these forms the work is driven by a range of
applications: provenance and intellectual property (IP)
\citep{horwitz2024mother, zeng2024huref, nikolic2025provenance}, model poisoning and misalignment
\citep{cloud2025subliminal}, inheritance of traits from parent models
\citep{cloud2025subliminal, agents2026same}, and data inquiries and auditing
\citep{zhu2025independence}.

\textbf{Model parameters.} Some works study how changes in parameters due to fine-tuning or
reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF) in child models can be traced back to parent
models. MoTHer \citep{horwitz2024mother} recovers model family trees from weight similarity over
public checkpoints via a directed minimum spanning tree. \citet{yu2025neural} show that
parent--child and grandparent--child lineage can be recovered both with a learning-free approximation
of the fine-tuning step and with a learned detector, the latter exceeding $99\%$ accuracy. Beyond
direct lineage, \citet{ghostspec2025} show that similarly trained models carry spectral weight
``fingerprints'' that can identify common ancestry. Spectral DeTuning \citep{horwitz2024spectral}
further shows that base weights are recoverable from a set of shared low-rank adapter (LoRA)
variants, indicating that the full model set is not required to estimate ancestry. However, none of
these works recovers a full training-topology tree, nor evaluates against a known ground-truth
training tree.

Other works examine \emph{why} lineage carries from parent to
child. Models fine-tuned from a shared initialization remain within a single connected low-loss basin
\citep{wortsman2022model, frankle2020linear}, so pairwise weight distance is a meaningful proxy for
genealogical distance. The displacement a task induces the \emph{task vector}
$\Delta\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{ft}}-\theta_{\mathrm{base}}$ \citep{ilharco2023editing}.  Across different tasks, these task vectors are near-orthogonal, so
distinct lineages contribute largely independent directions rather than canceling. Centered Kernel
Alignment (CKA) \citep{kornblith2019similarity} is a standard tool for comparing trained models. Low-rank adaptation \citep{hu2022lora} and
intrinsic-dimensionality results \citep{aghajanyan2021intrinsic} explain why a small update suffices
and why the \emph{rank} of that update bounds how much lineage is legible, while near-orthogonal task
subspaces have been linked to catastrophic forgetting \citep{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}. 

\textbf{Model parameters $\leftrightarrow$ response output.} A second group connects how changes in parameters produce differences in behavior. \citet{cloud2025subliminal} show that child models inherit
``subliminal'' traits from a parent even when every reference to the trait is filtered out of the training text, and prove that under suitable conditions (a large enough training signal) a parent's preference is preserved with respect to the trait's loss---provided the parent and child share an initialization. Representation-level methods sit here too: REEF \citep{zhang2025reef} compares the internal representations (via CKA) of a suspect and a victim model to decide derivation, and gradient-response fingerprints \citep{tensorguard2025} classify model families from how parameters react to probe inputs. These works establish that parameter proximity tracks behavioral and
representational similarity, but only qualitatively and pairwise---none predicts behavioral similarity on a \emph{held-out} task under a controlled tree, which is precisely what our behavioral bridge does
(\S\ref{sec:exp:behavioral}): we withhold an entire task from training and test whether weight distance still predicts output similarity.

\textbf{Response output.} The most studied track infers lineage from behavior alone. Its appeal is
practical: it is learning-free, requiring only inference rather than further training, and---unlike
weight comparison, which typically needs matching architectures and parameter layouts---it needs only
text or benchmark outputs to compare \citep{yax2025phylolm, wu2026llmdna}. PhyloLM
\citep{yax2025phylolm} builds phylogenetic trees of LLMs from output similarity and predicts benchmark
performance, while LLM DNA \citep{wu2026llmdna} defines a functional ``DNA'' representation that carries
lineage; both reconstruct phylogenies over hundreds of models. Related output-only methods test
pairwise provenance from next-token distributions \citep{nikolic2025provenance} or via statistical
independence of model outputs \citep{zhu2025independence}. However, this line is typically unsupervised
and depends on model prompting or probes, with no known ground-truth topology to score against.

Our work fills this gap by building a full bridge from parameter-based lineage to behavioral lineage
while (1) attempting full training-tree topology recovery and (2) evaluating against ground-truth
training trees. This lets us quantify the link between changes in weights and changes in behavior,
and---mirroring the result of \citet{cloud2025subliminal}---we prove an analogous theorem: lineage is
recoverable exactly when the per-edge updates share a dominant direction (high coherence), the same
shared-initialization alignment that drives subliminal trait transfer.

\section{Framework}
\label{sec:framework}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

In this section, we define our terms and notation and establish the statistical framework underlying our experiments.


Much like \citep{wu2026llmdna}, we use the framework of phylogenies and LLM ``DNA'' to estimate important properties of parent-child training. We assume, unlike \cite{} but like \citep{yu2025neural}, that all node models are derived from a single root model. Unlike prior works, we focus not only on parent-child pairs but on recovering partial topology across all nodes, not just child-parent pairs.  We estimate the phylogeny by first estimating a distance matrix and then using neighbor-joining \citep{saitou1987neighbor} to recover the training tree.


\subsection{Phylogenetic Tree Recovery}
\label{sec:framework:phylo}
% We frame fine-tuning lineage recovery as a phylogenetics problem.
% Pairwise weight distances → NJ tree → RF vs ground truth → permutation p.
% Why NJ > directed MST (MoTHer): statistically principled, known properties.
% Cite: Saitou & Nei 1987 (NJ), Robinson & Foulds 1981 (RF).

To estimate $\widehat{\mathcal{P}}$, we use the following recipe:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Choose set of layers $\mathcal{L}$ and distance (or similarity) metric ($d$)
    \item For layer $L^{(\ell)}$, estimate the distance between the layer weights from model $i$ to model $j$,
    $$
    D_{i,j}^{(\ell)} = d(L^{(\ell)}_i, L^{(\ell)}_j)
    $$
    \item Assemble $\hat{\textbf{D}}$, where $\hat{\textbf{D}}$ is function of the $D_{i,j}^{(\ell)}$ distances such as the average distance of pairwise weights across all layers
    \item Estimate $\widehat{{\mathcal{P}}}$ using $f(\hat{\textbf{D}})$ where $f$ is the NJ method\footnote{We assume that the models in question are leaves and not internal nodes}
\end{enumerate}

Step (3) allows us to use different layers to achieve different or even multiple estimates of $\widehat{\mathcal{P}}$, which we can do to evaluate layer importance.  Since phylogeny-space grows super-exponentially in the number of leaves, exact topology recovery is unlikely.
Instead, researchers commonly use Robinson-Foulds (RF) distance \citep{robinson1981comparison} to measure how ``close'' an estimated phylogeny is to the true one, a measure of partial topology recovery. An RF distance of $k$ means the two trees disagree on $k$ bipartitions, splits present in one tree but absent in the other.
A maximum RF distance can be computed from the total number of leaves, and the ratio (normalized RF) enables comparison across trees of different sizes. A normalized RF near 0 indicates near-perfect recovery; near 1 indicates a tree no better than random.

We assess significance via a permutation test. Let $\mathrm{RF}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}},
\mathcal{P}^*)$ denote the Robinson-Foulds distance between the estimated and
ground-truth trees for a given run. The null hypothesis is that $\widehat{\mathcal{P}}$
carries no lineage information:
$$
H_0: \mathrm{RF}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}, \mathcal{P}^*) \ge
     \mathrm{RF}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{rand}}, \mathcal{P}^*)
$$
where $\mathcal{P}_{\text{rand}}$ is drawn uniformly from the space of binary trees
on $n$ leaves. We approximate this null distribution with 1{,}000 random trees per
run, yielding a permutation $p$-value for each run. To combine evidence across the
50 independent runs, we apply Fisher's method: $-2\sum_{i=1}^{50} \log p_i \sim
\chi^2(100)$ under the global null.

\subsection{Distance Metric and Shared-Direction Coherence}
\label{sec:framework:metric}

One question we sought to answer is what distance (or similarity) metric should we use to compare layers of weights to one another.  We decided to use vector-based norms as opposed to matrix or array based norms for easier portability across architectures.  The distances we considered are  cosine, correlation, $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ (the latter of which was used in MoTHER \cite{}).  The first two distances are bounded by 1 and so the distances are comparable across different layers.  The second two distances are unbounded, which can allow different magnitudes of weights to dominate the distance.

We use cosine distance throughout: it is bounded and scale-invariant across layers, so no single high-magnitude tensor (e.g.\ the noisy layer norms) dominates, unlike the unbounded $\ell_1$/$\ell_2$. A per-metric signal-to-noise comparison corroborating this choice---cosine and correlation separate same- from cross-branch distances far better than $\ell_1$/$\ell_2$---is given in Appendix~\ref{app:snr}.

We select the distance metric using the same quantity our framework turns on: the shared-direction coherence $c=\lambda_1(S)/n$ (defined in \S\ref{sec:framework:theory}), the fraction of update energy along a single dominant direction. Coherence is a meaningful fraction only for a bounded, scale-invariant similarity. Table~\ref{tab:coherence} reports $c$ by metric over \texttt{branching\_v3}: cosine and correlation place nearly all update energy on one shared lineage direction (cosine highest), while the unbounded $\ell_1$/$\ell_2$ distances are magnitude-dominated and admit no valid decomposition. We therefore use cosine distance for the remainder of the paper, and read the same coherence off the cosine updates to predict \emph{which training conditions} admit topology recovery (\S\ref{sec:results:weighttree}, Fig.~\ref{fig:coherence_recovery}).

\subsection{Per Layer Estimated Phylogenies}
\label{sec:framework:layers}
% Each weight tensor → its own NJ tree.
% Distribution of per-layer RF distances is the data; consensus tree is summary.
% More statistically principled than any competitor — flag explicitly.

One contribution we have in our methodology is the use of per-layer distance matrices between models as independent estimates as opposed to using the total distance across all layers.  The inspiration for this analysis is how biological phylogenies are estimated across multiple genes, the assumption being that different genes encode different information on how changes are occurring \cite{}.

Thus, a model with $L$ layers can result in $L$ distance matrices.  These distance matrices can either be used to each form their own estimate of $\hat{P}$ resulting in a collection of estimates.  From that set, a conensus phylogeny (i.e. the most common tree) is generally taken as the estimate.  Another alternative is to use a function of the distance matrices, such as the mean, to have a less noisy estimate of $\hat{\textbf{D}}$, to form $\widehat{\mathcal{P}}$. In addition to giving us a less variable estimate of the phylogeny, this layer-wise distance allows us to identify the most and least important layers in training by examining which layers change the most or least across multiple training runs.


\subsection{When Lineage Is Recoverable: Separation, and Coherence as Its Proxy}
\label{sec:framework:theory}

We give a statistical account of \emph{when} the recipe of \S\ref{sec:framework:phylo} succeeds.
Fine-tuning a child from its parent adds a displacement in weight space, which we model as a shared,
task-systematic direction (a ``task vector'') plus independent run-to-run noise: siblings trained on
similar data share more of that direction, unrelated branches less. The pairwise weight distances we
feed to neighbor-joining are therefore a lineage \emph{signal}---how the shared directions differ along
the tree---buried in \emph{noise} that accumulates over the connecting edges. Recovery comes down to one
latent quantity, the \emph{separation}: how far the distances place siblings from non-siblings relative
to that noise. The formal model and all proofs are deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}; here we state
the consequences and how we measure them.

\paragraph{Two regimes.} ``Reading the lineage'' hides two questions of very different difficulty.
\emph{Ordering} asks only whether same-branch pairs are closer than cross-branch pairs \emph{on
average}; it pools many comparisons, so even a tiny per-pair signal accumulates into a detectable
trend. \emph{Topology} asks to reconstruct the entire tree, which needs the local relationships right
\emph{everywhere at once}---a few wrong calls anywhere break it. We obtain the topology result
\emph{without} the tree-additivity condition neighbor-joining nominally needs (and that near-orthogonal
updates violate), by reasoning about \emph{rooted triples}---ordinal facts of the form ``$i$ and $j$
branched off together more recently than $k$''---which the classical \textsc{Build} algorithm
\citep{aho1981build} assembles into the unique tree using only the \emph{order} of distances, never
their exact values.

\begin{assumption}[Lineage monotonicity]\label{asm:mono}
Read a rooted triple $ij\,|\,k$ as the ancestry statement that $i$ and $j$ share a more recent common
ancestor than either shares with $k$---so $i,j$ are the \emph{siblings} and $k$ the \emph{outgroup}. We
assume only that this ancestral order is reflected in expected weight distance: the siblings are
expected to be closer than either is to the outgroup,
\[ \E[D_{ij}]<\E[D_{ik}] \quad\text{and}\quad \E[D_{ij}]<\E[D_{jk}], \]
for every true triple of the tree; write $\gamma$ for the smallest of these expected gaps (the
\emph{margin}). Tree-additivity implies this but not conversely---it constrains only the \emph{order} of
distances, not their values---so it can hold even in the near-orthogonal regime where additivity fails,
which is why our results never need the metric to behave like tree branch lengths.
\end{assumption}

\begin{theorem}[Ordering---the easy regime]\label{thm:order}
Pool $M$ \emph{comparable contrasts}, each a same-branch distance differenced against a cross-branch
distance whose labels are exchangeable under the permutation null (Definition~\ref{def:contrast},
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}), so the difference is mean-zero under that null. With margin $\gamma$ and
contrast variance at most $V$, the ``same-branch closer'' test (our permutation, Mann--Whitney, and
within-run $r$ statistics) has power tending to $1$ once $M\gamma^2/V\gg1$. Because it pools, ordering
is detected even when the per-pair separation is small.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Topology is harder.} Recovering the full tree needs every local relationship right at once,
so it is governed by the \emph{worst} per-triple separation, not a pooled average. It is nonetheless
identifiable from ordinal information alone: once the worst per-triple separation clears $\approx 3\ln n$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:topo}, Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}), all rooted triples are called correctly and
\textsc{Build} \citep{aho1981build} reconstructs the exact tree with no additivity assumption. We do not
run \textsc{Build}: the pipeline reconstructs with neighbor-joining and we report topology recovery
empirically (\S\ref{sec:results}). The two regimes therefore dissociate---at intermediate separation,
pooling keeps \emph{ordering} detectable while \emph{topology} stays out of reach, exactly the
decoder-only Llama regime (Appendix~\ref{app:llama}).

\paragraph{Measuring it: shared-direction coherence.} The separation is latent---it depends on the
unknown signal and noise. What we can compute from the weights alone is the \emph{shared-direction
coherence} of the trained-module updates: from a run's pairwise cosine-similarity matrix
$S=\mathbf{1}-D$, define
\[
  c \;=\; \lambda_1(S)/n \;\in\; [\,1/n,\;1\,],
\]
the fraction of update energy along a single dominant direction ($c=1$ when all sibling updates are
parallel, $c=1/n$ when mutually orthogonal). Under the generative model $c$ is an increasing function of
the signal fraction and hence of the separation (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs},
Proposition~\ref{prop:coh}), so we use it as a label-free proxy for whether a run is in the recoverable
regime. \textbf{Two honest cautions.} Coherence summarizes alignment but not whether the residual
structure is tree-shaped, so it is \emph{not} sufficient on its own: a maximally coherent ``star'' of
parallel updates has $c\to1$ yet carries no tree to recover (Proposition~\ref{prop:coh}). What we claim
is therefore \emph{conditional}---\emph{under} lineage monotonicity (Assumption~\ref{asm:mono}), higher
coherence places more of each update along the lineage direction and raises the separation toward the
topology threshold.

\paragraph{Empirically, coherence separates the regimes.} Across our experiments $c$ is sharply
bimodal: groups that recover the topology---Flan-T5 (full FT and LoRA), Pythia-160M (full FT), and
full-FT Llama-3.2-1B---sit at $c\approx0.99$ (updates nearly parallel), while every diluted low-rank
decoder group (Llama-8B and 1B LoRA) sits at $c\approx0.20$ (updates nearly orthogonal), with nothing
in between (Fig.~\ref{fig:coherence_recovery}). A single threshold on $c$ separates the recovering from
the dead groups across all of them. We caution that our groups vary the update dimension together with
coherence, so they cannot \emph{isolate} coherence from other dimension-linked explanations; what they
establish is that the two regimes are cleanly told apart by one label-free, weight-only quantity. Low
rank is then one of several mechanisms---alongside large width, fused projections, and near-floor
displacement---that drive coherence down.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
We structure our experiments around three questions: whether weight changes
recover training lineage, whether they predict downstream behavior, and
whether family structure is visible from behavioral outputs alone in
frontier models without weight access.

\subsection{Controlled Branching Trees}
\label{sec:exp:trees}
% Base: Flan-T5-base (250M). Bernoulli branching, 4 tasks, 50 replications,
% 5--8 leaves, known topology by construction.
% 2000 steps, lr=3e-4, batch=8, grad_accum=4, bf16.
% Controlled design enables RF evaluation — all competitors lack this.

To create a controlled experiment, we generate stochastic phylogenies or training trees, beginning from a root model.  The tree generating process is described in detail in Appenidx \ref{app:details}.  Our generating process produces fairly balanced phylogenies with up to 8 leaf nodes.  We generate 50 such phylogenies, each constituting the basic structure for a single training tree. We then assign task/dataset pairs to each edge. At each split in the tree, a child model is fine-tuned from its parent model on a random task/data set pair.  First a training task is randomly sampled and then a dataset within that task is randomly sampled.  The resulting pair is assigned to that edge.

%% This probably gets moved to intro or somewhere -- it's a real scenario
The motivation for this controlled experiment is from an industry-type training where a base model is given to different departments and each department is able to perform its own fine tuning on a model.  A question may arise if a new department begins and is interested in using an already fine-tuned model on a new task.  Which one should they pick?

The generated phylogenies are then used as fine-tuning instructions, the details of which can be found in Appendix [add-fine-tuning-instructions].  At each node in the phylogeny, a new model is trained and we can access each \textit{leaf} model's weights. 

\subsection{Do weight changes carry model lineage?}
\label{sec:exp:weights}

Once we have the trained leaf models, we can use the steps in Section \ref{sec:framework:phylo} to estimate a phylogeny from the weight changes.  Since we know the actual training trees, we then can use RF distance to compare how far the estimated phylogeny is from the known training tree, unlike previous works.  We repeat this process for each generated phylogeny.

In our experiments, we vary the root model, task types, and which layers we train. We examine both an encoder-decoder model and a decoder-only model: Flan-T5-base (250M parameters) and Llama-3.1-8B, respectively. The tasks we fine-tune on include Q\&A, classification, translation, and text summarization. For Flan-T5-base, we run both full fine-tuning and four LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} adapter configurations varying the scope of trained parameters: key projection matrices only, query/key/value projections, all four attention projections (including output), and all projection matrices. Llama-3.1-8B serves as both a decoder-only and entry into more frontier-like models.  Llama-3.1-8B is trained with the key-only and query/key/value LoRA configurations; full fine-tuning is omitted at this scale due to memory constraints. Detailed training hyperparameters for all conditions are in Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\subsection{Can Weight Changes Predict Behavior?}
\label{sec:exp:behavioral}

While recovering ancestral information about a model is important for
provenance and auditing, a practical question is whether weight-space
proximity predicts anything useful about model behavior. Here we estimate
how strongly whitebox weight distance predicts behavioral similarity on a
probe task, after absorbing run-level variation.

We operationalize behavioral similarity in two ways: task performance
(ROUGE-L) and semantic similarity between model outputs measured via
sentence-embedding cosine distance, with the latter as our primary
dependent variable. In both cases we fit a fixed-effects regression of
behavioral similarity on weight distance with a per-run fixed intercept
(run fixed effects, which absorb run-level variation), and report the
\emph{standardized} coefficient $\beta$---both weight distance and the
behavioral outcome $z$-scored within run. So standardized, $\beta$ equals
the within-run correlation $r$ and is the one effect size comparable across
conditions, since the raw slope inherits each condition's output variance
(see Appendix~\ref{app:regression} for the full specification, the raw and
semi-standardized coefficients, and sensitivity analyses).

For Flan-T5-base, we use translation as the probe task, training models
on summarization, QA, and classification only and withholding translation
as the holdout. For Llama-3.1-8B, we use HellaSwag --- a commonsense
sentence-completion benchmark --- as the held-out probe. The probes differ
across architectures because Flan-T5-base lacks the base commonsense
ability required for HellaSwag, producing degenerate outputs regardless
of fine-tuning condition (see Appendix~\ref{app:translation_probe}).
Llama-3.1-8B, by contrast, retains strong commonsense ability after
LoRA fine-tuning, making HellaSwag a meaningful held-out test of whether
weight proximity predicts behavioral similarity beyond the training
distribution.

\subsection{Behavior from Blackbox, Frontier Models}
\label{sec:exp:blackbox}
% GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama probed via output embeddings across 4 tasks.
% Family structure recoverable when aggregating tasks.
% Single-task confounded by formatting (verbosity, markdown).
% Translation cleanest — language crossing decouples embedding from source content.

Sometimes model weights are unavailable, and provenance must be inferred from behavioral outputs alone. Recent work has shown that frontier model families cluster meaningfully in behavioral embedding space
\citep{wu2026llmdna, yax2025phylolm}. We extend this line of work by probing 16 frontier models (GPT, Claude, Gemini, and open-weight families including Llama and Mistral) across four task types --- summarization, QA, classification, and translation --- using the same neighbor joining pipeline applied to pairwise sentence-embedding distances between model outputs. This design allows us to examine how much the recovered family structure depends
on task choice, and whether aggregating across tasks recovers structure that
single-task probes miss.

\section{Proofs: Separation, Identifiability, and the Coherence Proxy}
\label{app:proofs}

We restate and prove the results of \S\ref{sec:framework:theory}. Recall that
$w_i-w_j=\bsig_{ij}+\bnoise_{ij}$, where the deterministic \emph{signal} $\bsig_{ij}$ is the sum of
task vectors along the two root-to-leaf paths and the \emph{noise} $\bnoise_{ij}=\sum_e\pm\beps_e$
sums the independent mean-zero edge noises over the $\ell_{ij}$ edges separating $i$ and $j$. A rooted
triple $ij\,|\,k$ is the purely ordinal statement that $\{i,j\}$ branched off together more recently
than $k$; it is the atom of tree structure, and \textsc{Build} \citep{aho1981build} reconstructs the
unique tree displaying a complete consistent set of correct triples using only orderings of distances,
never their exact values.

\paragraph{Model.} Fine-tuning node $v$ from its parent adds $\Delta_v=\bd_{\tau(v)}+\beps_v$: a task
vector $\bd_{\tau}$ (the average direction in which task $\tau$ moves the model; children inherit their
ancestors' task vectors by summation down the tree) plus independent mean-zero run noise $\beps_v$ with
$\E\norm{\beps_v}^2=\nu$. Distinct task vectors share a common length $\norm{\bd}$ and pairwise cosine
$\rho<1$. We observe the $n$ leaves and their squared distances $D_{ij}=\norm{w_i-w_j}^2$. With
$X=D_{\text{cross}}-D_{\text{same}}$ and $\gamma$ the margin of Assumption~\ref{asm:mono}, we call
$\beta=\gamma^2/\Var[X]$ the \emph{separation} of a comparison---the squared margin relative to the
contrast variance. (We use ``separation'' throughout in place of the overloaded ``signal-to-noise
ratio.'')

\begin{definition}[Comparable contrast]\label{def:contrast}
A \emph{comparable contrast} is a matched pair $(D^{\text{same}},D^{\text{cross}})$ whose same/cross
labels are exchangeable under the permutation null, so that $X=D^{\text{cross}}-D^{\text{same}}$ is
mean-zero under the null and has $\E[X]\ge\gamma$ under the truth. Theorem~\ref{thm:order} pools $M$
such contrasts; the exchangeable matching---not any condition on the edge counts $\ell$---is what makes
the pooled permutation test valid.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Expected distance]\label{lem:mean}
$\E[D_{ij}]=\norm{\bsig_{ij}}^2+\nu\,\ell_{ij}$, where $\bsig_{ij}$ is the deterministic sum of task
vectors on the two root-paths and $\ell_{ij}$ is the number of edges separating $i$ and $j$. No
tree-additivity is used.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Each extra branch adds a fixed margin]\label{lem:margin}
For the canonical triple $ij\,|\,k$, averaging over task assignments,
$\E[D_{ij}]=2(1-\rho)\norm{\bd}^2+2\nu$ and $\E[D_{ik}]=(3-2\rho)\norm{\bd}^2+3\nu$, so the gap is
$\gamma=\norm{\bd}^2+\nu$ (the alignment $\rho$ cancels; it sets only how tight siblings are).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:mean}]
Since $\bsig_{ij}$ is deterministic,
$D_{ij}=\norm{\bsig_{ij}}^2+2\langle\bsig_{ij},\bnoise_{ij}\rangle+\norm{\bnoise_{ij}}^2$. The noise
runs over the $\ell_{ij}$ edges on the two root-paths, each term independent and mean zero, so
$\E\langle\bsig_{ij},\bnoise_{ij}\rangle=\langle\bsig_{ij},\E\bnoise_{ij}\rangle=0$ and, by
independence, $\E\norm{\bnoise_{ij}}^2=\sum_e\E\norm{\beps_e}^2=\nu\,\ell_{ij}$. Summing the three
expectations gives $\E[D_{ij}]=\norm{\bsig_{ij}}^2+\nu\,\ell_{ij}$; nothing about tree-additivity is
used.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:margin}]
Place the canonical triple so $i,j$ are leaves below the branch node $b$, and $k$ joins at
$a=\mathrm{pa}(b)$. Take distinct task vectors of common length $\norm{\bd}$ and pairwise cosine
$\rho$, i.e.\ $\langle\bd_\tau,\bd_{\tau'}\rangle=\rho\norm{\bd}^2$ for $\tau\neq\tau'$. The
same-branch signal is $\bsig_{ij}=\bd_i-\bd_j$ (the shared path up to $b$ cancels), so
$\norm{\bsig_{ij}}^2=2(1-\rho)\norm{\bd}^2$ and $\ell_{ij}=2$; Lemma~\ref{lem:mean} gives
$\E[D_{ij}]=2(1-\rho)\norm{\bd}^2+2\nu$. The cross-branch signal is $\bsig_{ik}=(\bd_b+\bd_i)-\bd_k$,
so
\[
\norm{\bsig_{ik}}^2=3\norm{\bd}^2+2\big(\langle\bd_b,\bd_i\rangle-\langle\bd_b,\bd_k\rangle-\langle\bd_i,\bd_k\rangle\big)
=(3-2\rho)\norm{\bd}^2,
\]
with $\ell_{ik}=3$, hence $\E[D_{ik}]=(3-2\rho)\norm{\bd}^2+3\nu$. Subtracting,
$\gamma=\big[(3-2\rho)-2(1-\rho)\big]\norm{\bd}^2+\nu=\norm{\bd}^2+\nu$; the $\rho$-terms cancel, and
the $jk$ comparison is identical by symmetry.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:order}]
For comparable contrasts $m=1,\dots,M$ (Definition~\ref{def:contrast}) put
$X_m=D^{\mathrm{cross}}_m-D^{\mathrm{same}}_m$, so $\E[X_m]\ge\gamma>0$ by Assumption~\ref{asm:mono} and
$\Var[X_m]\le V$, with average separation $\bar\beta:=\gamma^2/V$. The pooled mean
$\bar X=\frac1M\sum_m X_m$ has $\E[\bar X]\ge\gamma$; treating the contrasts as weakly dependent (they
share leaves and edges, so this is an approximation rather than exact independence),
$\Var[\bar X]\lesssim V/M$, and the standardized statistic $Z=\bar X/\sqrt{\Var[\bar X]}$ obeys
$\E[Z]\gtrsim\sqrt{M\bar\beta}$. Under the null of exchangeable same/cross labels $\E[X_m]=0$ and $Z$ is
centered and asymptotically Gaussian, so a one-sided level-$\alpha$ test has power
$\Phi\!\big(\sqrt{M\bar\beta}-z_\alpha\big)\to1$ whenever $M\bar\beta\to\infty$. The permutation,
Mann--Whitney, and within-run $r$ statistics are monotone in $\bar X$ and inherit the power.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Topology identifiability]\label{thm:topo}
Let $\beta^\ast$ be the worst single-comparison separation $\gamma^2/V_Y$ over all triples. Each triple
is called correctly with probability at least $1-2e^{-c_0\beta^\ast}$, so all $2\binom{n}{3}$
comparisons hold together with probability at least $1-n^3 e^{-c_0\beta^\ast}$; on that event
\textsc{Build} reconstructs $\mathcal{P}^\ast$ exactly. Hence topology is identifiable once
$\beta^\ast\gtrsim 3\ln n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:topo}]
Fix a triple $ij\,|\,k$ and one of its comparisons, $Y=D_{ik}-D_{ij}$, with $\E[Y]\ge\gamma$
(Assumption~\ref{asm:mono}). Writing each squared distance as
$D=\norm{\bsig}^2+2\langle\bsig,\bnoise\rangle+\norm{\bnoise}^2$, its random part
$2\langle\bsig,\bnoise\rangle+(\norm{\bnoise}^2-\E\norm{\bnoise}^2)$ is a linear-plus-quadratic form in
the sub-Gaussian edge noise, hence sub-exponential (Bernstein / Hanson--Wright) with scale controlled
by $\nu$ and $\norm{\bsig}$. So $Y-\E[Y]$ is sub-exponential with variance proxy $V_Y$, and
Bernstein's inequality gives
\[
\Pr[Y\le 0]\le\exp\!\Big(-c_0\min\{\E[Y]^2/V_Y,\ \E[Y]/b\}\Big)\le e^{-c_0\beta^\ast}
\]
in the moderate-deviation regime where $\beta^\ast=\E[Y]^2/V_Y$ controls the bound. A triple is
miscalled only if its $ik$- or $jk$-comparison is non-positive, so its error is at most
$2e^{-c_0\beta^\ast}$. Unioning over the $\binom{n}{3}$ triples, the chance that any triple is miscalled
is at most $2\binom{n}{3}e^{-c_0\beta^\ast}\le n^3e^{-c_0\beta^\ast}$. On the complementary event every
rooted triple is correct; this set is complete and tree-consistent, so \textsc{Build}
\citep{aho1981build} returns the unique tree displaying them, namely $\mathcal{P}^*$. Finally
$n^3e^{-c_0\beta^\ast}\le\delta$ iff $\beta^\ast\ge(3\ln n-\ln\delta)/c_0$. The constants $c_0,b$ depend
on the sub-Gaussian noise parameters; this is an order-of-magnitude threshold, not a tight bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Ordering/topology dissociation]\label{cor:diss}
If $\beta^\ast\ll 3\ln n\ll M\bar\beta$, ordering is detected (Theorem~\ref{thm:order}) but topology is
not recovered: more orthogonal updates can keep ordering alive while starving topology.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:diss}]
In the stated range $M\bar\beta\gg1$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:order} gives detection power $\to1$, while
$\beta^\ast\lesssim 3\ln n$ fails the sufficient condition of Theorem~\ref{thm:topo}, so the topology
guarantee does not apply.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Coherence proxies the separation, conditionally]\label{prop:coh}
Model the $n$ trained-module updates as $\Delta_v=\bsig_v+\beps_v$ with shared-direction energy
$\norm{\bsig}^2$, independent noise $\E\norm{\beps_v}^2=\nu$, in dimension $d\gg n$, and let
$f=\norm{\bsig}^2/(\norm{\bsig}^2+\nu)$ be the update signal fraction. Then the expected cosine of two
normalized updates concentrates on a multiple of $f$, the cosine-similarity matrix $S$ is
equicorrelation to leading order, and
\[
  c=\lambda_1(S)/n=f+\tfrac{1-f}{n}+o(1),
\]
so $c$ is increasing in $f$ and---\emph{under} Assumption~\ref{asm:mono}, where larger $f$ raises the
margin $\gamma$---increasing in the separation $\beta$. The conditioning is essential. Coherence
measures alignment, not whether the residual is tree-shaped: a ``star'' of $n$ updates all equal to one
direction plus small noise has $c\to1$ but violates Assumption~\ref{asm:mono} (all pairs equidistant in
expectation) and carries no recoverable topology. Hence high coherence is necessary-type evidence for
the recoverable regime, \emph{not} a sufficient condition; we use it only as a label-free proxy,
validated empirically (\S\ref{sec:results:weighttree}).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch of Proposition~\ref{prop:coh}]
Writing $\hat g_v=\Delta_v/\norm{\Delta_v}$, the numerator
$\E\langle\Delta_v,\Delta_w\rangle=\langle\bsig_v,\bsig_w\rangle$ (cross-noise and signal--noise terms
vanish in expectation), while $\norm{\Delta_v}\to\sqrt{\norm{\bsig}^2+\nu}$ by concentration in
$d\gg n$; their ratio is a multiple of $f$. With equal off-diagonal $f$, $S$ has leading eigenvalue
$1+(n-1)f$ on the all-ones vector, giving $c=\lambda_1/n=f+(1-f)/n$. The off-diagonal in fact carries
the task-vector alignment $\rho$ ($\langle\bsig_v,\bsig_w\rangle=\rho\norm{\bsig}^2$ for siblings), so
$c$ conflates alignment with the noise fraction, and the equicorrelation form is leading-order (real
trees are block-structured), so the identity is approximate. The non-sufficiency claim is immediate:
the star satisfies the update law but not Assumption~\ref{asm:mono}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The estimator gap.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:topo} is an \emph{identifiability} statement: it proves the tree is determined by
ordinal (triple) information alone, with \textsc{Build} \citep{aho1981build} as the constructive
witness. \textsc{Build} is a proof device, not the estimator our pipeline runs: we reconstruct with
neighbor-joining, which relies on approximate tree-additivity (the four-point condition). So the
theorem guarantees the lineage signal is \emph{present} in recoverable ordinal form; it does not
guarantee neighbor-joining \emph{extracts} it, and the two can disagree precisely in the
near-orthogonal regime where additivity has decayed. The strictly monotone-invariant estimator is the
rooted triple itself --- $ij\,|\,k=\arg\min\{D_{ij},D_{ik},D_{jk}\}$, unchanged by any monotone
transform of $D$. We verified the invariance: rebuilding from $D$, $D^3$, and $e^{5D}-1$ gives the
identical tree every run ($\mathrm{RF}=0$), whereas neighbor-joining moves by up to $\mathrm{RF}=6$
under the same transforms. We therefore keep neighbor-joining as the primary estimator and use the
rooted-triple \textsc{Build} reconstructor as a strictly-ordinal cross-check.

\paragraph{From the separation to a measurable quantity.}
The theorems are governed by the lineage-informative fraction of the update. A rank-$r$ adapter in a
width-$d$ model can move the model only inside an $r$-dimensional slice, which retains a fraction
$r/d$ of any fixed signal direction (for a uniformly random $r$-subspace $V$, $\E[P_V]=(r/d)I$, so
$\E\norm{P_V x}^2=r/d$ for any unit $x$). This makes $s=r/d$ \emph{one} mechanism that lowers the
separation---not the only one, and not directly observable per run. Our experiments instead read the
shared-direction coherence $c=\lambda_1(S)/n$ (\S\ref{sec:framework:theory}), a label-free proxy for
the separation (Proposition~\ref{prop:coh}) that, unlike $s=r/d$, cleanly separates the recovering from
the dead groups (Fig.~\ref{fig:coherence_recovery}, Appendix~\ref{app:llama})---with the caveat of
Proposition~\ref{prop:coh} that coherence is a conditional proxy, not a sufficient condition.


\section{Experimental Details}
\label{app:details}

\subsection*{Tree Generation}
\label{app:tree-gen}

Training trees are generated via a Poisson branching process as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The root node (pretrained Flan-T5-base) draws $k \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda = 1.5)$ children, with a minimum of 1.
    \item Each subsequent node draws $k \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda = 1.5)$ children. Nodes that draw $k = 0$ naturally become leaves --- no separate pruning step is needed.
    \item Breadth First Search expansion continues until either a node budget ($n_\mathrm{nodes} = 14$ training nodes, not counting the root) is exhausted or a maximum depth ($n_\mathrm{layers} = 4$) is reached. The budget cap creates the truncation; any node alive when the budget runs out becomes a leaf.
    \item Each non-root node is assigned a task and dataset sampled without replacement from 4 task types (summarization, classification, QA, translation) and 36 datasets (9 summarization, 10 classification, 8 QA, 9 translation). The topology (parent--child structure) is fixed by a topology seed; dataset assignments vary across the 50 replications.
    \item Runs with fewer than 4 leaves are excluded from analysis, as trees with 3 or fewer leaves have trivial topology with no meaningful RF distance.
\end{enumerate}

This procedure yields trees with 4--8 observed leaves across the 50 replications, with variable depth and branching structure. The ground-truth topology is fully known by construction, enabling exact RF evaluation.

\subsection*{Training Configuration}
Base model: Flan-T5-base (250M parameters). Each node trained for 2,000 steps, learning rate $3 \times 10^{-4}$, batch size 8, gradient accumulation 4 (effective batch 32), warmup 200 steps, weight decay 0.01, bf16 precision. Up to 10,000 training examples and 1,000 evaluation examples per dataset, fixed dataset seed across all nodes to avoid data variation as a confound.

\section{Behavioral Regression: Full Results}
\label{app:regression}

All models regress behavioral output similarity (sentence-embedding cosine, translation probe
unless noted) on cosine weight distance with run fixed effects and SEs clustered on run. We report
three coefficients per condition (Table~\ref{tab:regression_betas}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Raw $\beta$} --- the unscaled slope. Significant and negative everywhere, but
    \emph{not} comparable across conditions: LoRA groups have far smaller absolute cosine distances,
    so their raw $\beta$ is inflated by orders of magnitude (e.g.\ $-52$ for full\_ft\_approx vs.\
    $-0.19$ for k\_only) without implying a larger effect.
  \item \textbf{Semi-standardized $\beta$} --- predictor $z$-scored within run; reads as ``$\Delta$
    output similarity per 1 within-run SD of weight distance.'' Interpretable within a condition,
    but still not cross-comparable because conditions differ in how much their \emph{outputs} vary
    (k\_only translations are near-identical, so its semi-standardized $\beta$ is tiny despite the
    strongest correlation).
  \item \textbf{Standardized $\beta$ (within-run)} --- both sides $z$-scored within run. This is the
    only cross-condition-comparable effect size, and it equals the within-run correlation. It is the
    number reported in the main text.
\end{itemize}

\section{Decoder-Only at Scale: Llama and the Coherence Bottleneck}
\label{app:llama}

We include decoder-only Llama models (3.1-8B, 3.2-1B-Instruct) as a robustness check at frontier
scale. All groups are cloned from the \texttt{branching\_v3} topologies (identical trees, so RF is
directly comparable to Table~\ref{tab:rf_by_leaves}) and fine-tuned with LoRA at rank $r=8$;
analysis uses \textsc{trained\_only=1} and \texttt{--min-leaves 4}.

The headline is a \emph{dissociation}: the pairwise ordering signal survives the
encoder-decoder$\rightarrow$decoder-only switch, but full topology recovery does not.
Table~\ref{tab:llama_recovery} shows every Llama group beats the random null on the ordering test
(rank-biserial $r\approx0.20$--$0.25$, all Mann-Whitney $p<10^{-6}$), yet clade recovery sits at
$20$--$32\%$ and \emph{no} group ever beats the label permutation ($0\%$ significant
vs.\ label)---i.e.\ it recovers task-level clustering, not within-task lineage. Contrast the
encoder-decoder Flan-T5 full FT ($\approx90\%$ clade recovery, Table~\ref{tab:rf_by_leaves}) and
Flan LoRA qkv ($\approx83\%$).

The mechanism is low \emph{shared-direction coherence} (\S\ref{sec:framework:theory}): the LoRA
updates point in mutually near-orthogonal directions (qkv cosine distance mean $0.943$, jammed at the
ceiling), so the dominant-eigenvalue fraction $c=\lambda_1/n$ sits at $\approx0.20$ --- an essentially
isotropic Gram with no shared lineage axis --- versus $c\approx0.99$ for every group that recovers
topology (Flan, Pythia full FT, full-FT Llama-1B). The metric geometry NJ needs collapses, even as the
\emph{rank order} of distances stays informative, which is why the ordering test survives while
topology does not.

A natural hypothesis was that this is pure dimensional dilution of a fixed-rank update --- that
coherence, and with it recovery, should rise monotonically with the rank fraction $s=r/d$. A
qkv rank sweep on Llama-3.2-1B ($r\in\{2,4,8,16,32\}$, spanning Flan's operating point) falsifies the
clean version: clade recovery stays flat at $28$--$36\%$ with $0\%$ significant vs.\ the label
permutation across the whole range, and $r{=}32$ ($s=1.6\!\times\!10^{-2}$, past Flan's
$s\approx1.0\!\times\!10^{-2}$) still recovers under a third of what Flan does at lower $s$. So $s=r/d$
is one of several things that lower coherence, not its sole determinant; coherence, measured directly,
is the variable that gates recovery. The decisive control is the rank limit itself: lifting it to a
full-rank update on the same trees (full-FT Llama-3.2-1B) restores coherence to $\approx1.0$ and
recovers topology completely (every true clade, beats the label permutation;
Table~\ref{tab:rf_by_leaves}). The Llama LoRA dead zone is therefore the low-rank orthogonality
bottleneck, not decoder-ness or scale per se.


\section{Translation Probe Outputs for TaskHoldout Models}

the translation probe to test whether weight distance predicts behavioral similarity
even when the probe task was never trained. However, this condition reveals a
fundamental limitation of behavioral probing on out-of-distribution tasks: models
whose most recent fine-tuning was on classification have overwritten the base
model's translation ability entirely, producing degenerate single-token outputs
rather than translations.
