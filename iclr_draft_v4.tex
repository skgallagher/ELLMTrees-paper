\begin{abstract}
While over a million deep learning models are hosted online, most are fine-tuned descendants from a small set of ancestors.  The lineage of these models is only sometimes documented fully, even though provenance, auditing, and safe deployment all depend on proper documentation. In response to this issue, we hypothesize that fine-tuning acts as an evolutionary process that leaves signal in weight space, and show that a model \textit{phylogeny} (evolutionary tree) can be estimated from model weights alone and scored against a known ground-truth phylogeny, recovering all true clades (branches) in 70--93\% of runs.  We treat individual layers as gene-like estimators of lineage, revealing where evolutionary signal is concentrated and allowing each layer to produce its own phylogeny.  We are able to show that we can accurately estimate the phylogeny using about 3\% of Flan's scalar weights, suggesting that lineage can be recovered from a small, architecture-specific weight subset. Moreover, we show that this phylogeny estimation is associated with known mathematical concepts from phylogenetics: the Atteson margin and four-point additivity. Beyond phylogeny estimation, we report two additional empirical findings.  One, weight-space proximity in models predicts behavioral similarity on several held-out tasks about truthfulness, knowledge, reasoning, and common sense, though the strength and statistical support depend on the probe.  Two, weight estimation of phylogeny supports and can even outperform behavioral estimation of phylogeny such as in low-rank settings.  We finally compare our weight-based method to a state-of-the-art behavior-based method on a real, albeit small modern model phylogeny. On the topology-eligible subset of fresh matched controlled trees, weights recover 96\% of clades versus PhyloLM's 35\% under rank-8 LoRA and 97\% versus 52\% under full fine-tuning, while both methods recover all three documented splits in the real Mistral family.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}




While there are over one million models available on HuggingFace for deep learning and a handful of frontier
labs constantly releasing updated versions, most models are
fine-tuned descendants of a few ancestors.  Very few of those models transparently document their full model lineage. Benchmarks can reveal how a model performs on a task, but not how a model originated, how it differs from its sibling models, or how it may
fail. In contrast, training lineage can reveal much about model provenance, auditing, and safety~\citep{behavioral2026provenance, nikolic2026model, tensorguard2025}.

We, like other prior works~\citep{horwitz2025mother, yu2025neural, wu2026llmdna}, observe that fine-tuning is a path-dependent, branching process. Each
child model inherits its parent's  updated weights, nudged towards a certain loss and/or data set. This is
precisely an evolutionary process, as described in \cite{yax2025phylolm}, and moreover, the evolutionary path leaves signal in weight space. We treat a line or genealogy of fine-tuned models
as a phylogeny (i.e. an evolutionary tree~\cite{saitou1987neighbor}) and ask whether this phylogeny can be \emph{estimated} from the model weights alone.

As motivation, consider a firm that forks a base model into finance, IT, and fraud variants, each fine-tuned until
dozens of versions circulate with no recorded lineage (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}A). A practitioner needs
to know which to build on, where two versions diverged, and where each fails. Phylogenetic algorithms
were built for exactly this inference in biology~\citep{saitou1987neighbor, robinson1981comparison},
and we adapt them to neural net model weights: we estimate a phylogeny scored against a \emph{known}
ground-truth phylogeny (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}A), link the changes in weights to changes in behavior (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}B), and finally compare weight-based phylogenies to behavior-only estimates (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}C).

  that we read as a model phylogeny.}

Our primary contribution is that analyzing weight changes between fine-tunings allows accurate phylogeny estimation. We use a fitted Atteson diagnostic and label-free four-point additivity to characterize when recovery succeeds, while treating both as empirical diagnostics rather than guarantees. As a result of these weight-based estimates, we can further show two behavior-related consequences. In summary:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt,parsep=0pt]
  \item \textbf{Phylogeny estimation from model weights against ground truth.} Guided by the geometry of
    fine-tuning updates, our distance metric and summary-statistic choices estimate the model phylogeny from
    weights alone. Our controlled experiments recover all true clades in $70$--$93\%$ of runs
    ($87$--$98\%$ clade recovery). We evaluate against known ground-truth phylogenies and show that a subset containing about 3\% of the scalar weights can accurately estimate the known phylogenies. Treating layers as gene-like estimators also yields a separate phylogeny from each layer and localizes where lineage signal is carried. We show that our weight-based method accurately recovers all the splits in a small but real Mistral phylogeny.
  \item \textbf{Weight-behavior bridge.} Changes in weights can be used to predict similarity in model behavior.  In particular, we show that models that are closer in weight-space behave more similarly in behavior-space on \textit{held-out} tasks such as truthfulness, reasoning, knowledge, and common sense.
  \item \textbf{Controlled ground truth exposes when behavior-only lineage fails.}
    On the same 46 topology-eligible fresh controlled trees, weights recover 96\% of clades versus 35\% for
PhyloLM~\citep{yax2025phylolm} under rank-8 LoRA, and 97\% versus 52\% under full fine-tuning.
    On a documented Mistral family with large RLHF/DPO forks, both methods
    recover all three true splits. Thus behavior-only estimation can succeed after behaviorally large
    updates, while weight access remains substantially more informative for smaller updates and
    requires no output sampling.
\end{itemize}

Our main controlled topology results cover Flan-T5-base (250M) and Llama-3.2-1B, with both full
fine-tuning and variants of LoRA. Within architecture--training groups, the fitted Atteson diagnostic
has a moderate association with recovery, whereas label-free additivity is only weakly associated;
we therefore use both as descriptive diagnostics rather than trust guarantees.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

Deep learning lineage studies take three broad forms: \emph{model weights}, \emph{weight--behavior},
and \emph{behavior only}, driven by applications in provenance and IP
\citep{horwitz2025mother, zeng2024huref, nikolic2026model}, model poisoning and misalignment
\citep{cloud2025subliminal}, trait inheritance \citep{cloud2025subliminal, agents2026same}, and data
auditing \citep{zhu2025independence}. Table~\ref{tab:related} compares some of the closest methods.

\textbf{Model weights.} Several works trace weight changes from fine-tuning or reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF) back to parent models. MoTHer
\citep{horwitz2025mother} recovers directed model trees from weight similarity via a directed minimum
spanning tree and evaluates against controlled, known model hierarchies; \citet{yu2025neural} recover parent--child and grandparent--child lineage with both a
learning-free fine-tuning approximation and a learned detector ($>99\%$ accuracy). Beyond direct
lineage, spectral weight ``fingerprints'' identify common ancestry \citep{ghostspec2025}, and Spectral
DeTuning \citep{horwitz2024spectral} recovers base weights from shared LoRA variants. Our setting
differs from MoTHer's observed-node model tree: we observe only terminal checkpoints and estimate a
leaf phylogeny whose internal ancestors are latent.

Other works examine \emph{why} lineage is detectable in models. Models fine-tuned from a shared initialization stay
within a single low-error basin and are robust to SGD noise
\citep{neyshabur2020,wortsman2022model,frankle2020linear}, so pairwise weight distance can approximate genealogical distance. Additionally, task vectors \citep{ilharco2023editing} are near-orthogonal across tasks, so
distinct lineages contribute independent directions. LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} and intrinsic-dimensionality
results \citep{aghajanyan2021intrinsic} explain why small-rank updates suffice and why rank matters.

\textbf{Model weights $\leftrightarrow$ behavior.} A second group links weight changes to
behavior. \citet{cloud2025subliminal} show child models inherit ``subliminal'' traits even when every
reference to the trait is filtered from training text, and prove a parent's preference is preserved
under a large enough training signal given a shared initialization. REEF \citep{zhang2025reef} compares
internal representations (via centered kernel alignment, CKA~\citep{kornblith2019similarity}) to decide lineage, and gradient-response fingerprints
\citep{tensorguard2025} classify families from how weights react to probes. These establish that
weight closeness tracks behavioral similarity, but only pairwise, not as a full phylogeny.

\textbf{Behavior only.} An advantage of blackbox studies is they need only API access or benchmark outputs
\citep{yax2025phylolm, wu2026llmdna} as opposed to full model access. PhyloLM \citep{yax2025phylolm} builds LLM phylogenies from output
similarity and predicts benchmark performance; LLM DNA \citep{wu2026llmdna} defines a functional
``DNA'' representation carrying lineage; both reconstruct phylogenies over hundreds of models. Other
output-only methods test pairwise provenance from next-token distributions \citep{nikolic2026model} or
output independence \citep{zhu2025independence}. These methods are advantageous as they do not require shared
architecture and can compare models across families and sizes, an advantage weight-based methods currently lack.
These studies, however, are typically unsupervised and prompt-dependent, with no ground-truth topology to score
against.

We bridge weight- and behavior-based lineage, determine where the phylogeny
can be estimated, and evaluate against ground-truth trees. This makes explicit an analogy to
\citet{cloud2025subliminal}, whose guarantee that a parent's preference survives ``a large enough training
signal'' has a phylogenetic counterpart in Atteson's theorem: for a fixed additive tree metric,
neighbor-joining provably recovers the tree when distance error is less than half its shortest edge.

\section{Framework}
\label{sec:framework}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

We first define our terms, notation, and statistical framework. A \emph{phylogeny} $\mathcal{P}$ is a tree
whose leaves are the observed models and whose internal nodes are latent common ancestors; a
\emph{clade} is the set of leaves descending from one such ancestor (one branch of the tree). We analyze
single-root model families but observe only their terminal models. Thus, whereas MoTHer
\citep{horwitz2025mother} reconstructs a directed spanning tree over observed root, intermediate, and
terminal checkpoints, we estimate the unrooted leaf topology with internal ancestors latent. We form a pairwise distance matrix and apply neighbor-joining (NJ)
\citep{saitou1987neighbor} to estimate the phylogeny.

\textbf{Phylogeny estimation.}\label{sec:framework:phylo}
Assume we have a model $g(\theta)$ where $\theta$ is composed of multiple layers $\theta_\ell$. To estimate $\widehat{\mathcal{P}}$: (1) choose a set of layers $\theta_\mathcal{L}$ and a distance metric $d$;
(2) for each layer $\ell$ compute $D_{i,j}^{(\ell)} = d(\theta^{(\ell)}_i, \theta^{(\ell)}_j)$ between models
$i,j$; (3) assemble $\widehat{\mathbf{D}}$ as a function of the $D_{i,j}^{(\ell)}$ (e.g.\ the across-layer
average); and (4) estimate $\widehat{\mathcal{P}} = f(\widehat{\mathbf{D}})$ with $f$ as the NJ method.

Step (3) lets different layers yield different estimates of $\widehat{\mathcal{P}}$, which we use to
assess each layer's lineage signal. We score an estimated phylogeny against the known truth with the
Robinson--Foulds (RF) distance \citep{robinson1981comparison}, the number of bipartitions on which the
two trees disagree, which decomposes into false positives (FP) and false negatives (FN). Because NJ
returns strictly binary trees while our ground-truth phylogenies contain \textit{polytomies} (nodes with
more than two children), NJ is forced to add resolving edges. As a result, RF is never zero even for a correct binary
reconstruction. These forced edges inflate FP without being genuine errors. We therefore treat FN, the
number of true clades the reconstruction misses, as a more representative metric. Additionally, we report clade
recovery $= 1 - \mathrm{FN}/|\text{true splits}|$ and polytomy-aware exact recovery (PAER), the fraction of
runs with $\mathrm{FN}=0$, alongside RF. We also report the standardized within-run branch correlation
$r_{\mathrm{branch}}$ between weight distance and a same-branch indicator. We analyze only unrooted
phylogenies with at least four leaves because three leaves admit only one unrooted topology and no
nontrivial internal split.


\textbf{Distance metric.}\label{sec:framework:metric}
We flatten each weight tensor to a vector and compare models with vector distances rather than matrix
norms, for portability across architectures, considering cosine, correlation, $\ell_1$, and $\ell_2$
(the last used in MoTHer~\citep{horwitz2025mother}). We adopt cosine distance ($1$ minus cosine
similarity) throughout, for three reasons. It is bounded and scale-invariant across layers, so no single
high-magnitude layer dominates the aggregate, unlike $\ell_1$ or $\ell_2$. It is the prevailing choice in
recent provenance and fingerprinting work \citep{zeng2024huref, behavioral2026provenance,
zhu2025independence}, keeping our distances comparable to that literature. Finally, it has a direct geometric
reading on merged checkpoints: descendants whose weight vectors are deflected in similar directions remain closer in angle. Each child in our fresh LoRA trees trains a new adapter on its parent's merged weights. For analysis, we replay the ordered root-to-leaf adapter chain, merging each adapter into its parent state, and compute distances on the resulting standalone full-weight leaf checkpoints. Thus both LoRA and full-fine-tuning rows use deployable full model weights; the LoRA training intervention remains low-rank even though its evaluated checkpoint is merged.





\textbf{Per-layer estimated phylogenies.}
We treat each layer as a gene-like estimator rather than collapsing immediately to a single all-layer
distance, by analogy to biological phylogenies that must cohere across genes encoding different
information~\citep{maddison1997gene}. A model with $L$ layers therefore yields $L$ distance matrices and
$L$ independently estimated NJ trees. We either average the distance matrices before NJ or combine the
per-layer trees by their Billera--Holmes--Vogtmann (BHV) Fr\'echet mean, the tree minimizing the sum of
squared BHV geodesic distances~\citep{billera2001geometry, willis2019confidence}. This layer-wise view
both provides an aggregate estimate and lets us rank which layers recover the lineage well on their own
(\S\ref{sec:results:weighttree}).


\textbf{When estimation succeeds.}\label{sec:framework:theory}
Model lineage can be probed at two levels: pairwise ordering (e.g.\ whether two models sit on the same
branch) or whole-tree recovery, the former being easier. For ordering, we pool all pairwise model
comparisons within each valid run and test with the Mann--Whitney $U$ statistic. This branch-level
diagnostic is simpler than either pairwise parent--child detection~\citep{yu2025neural}, MoTHer's
directed observed-node tree recovery~\citep{horwitz2025mother}, or our latent-ancestor leaf topology. Recovering the full
\textit{phylogeny} is harder but well studied in phylogenetics: NJ is exact when the distance matrix is
additive, a condition model-weight distances satisfy only approximately. Atteson's theorem makes the
tolerance precise.
\begin{theorem}[\citealp{atteson1999performance}]\label{thm:atteson}
Let $D_T$ be the additive leaf metric of an edge-weighted binary tree $T$ whose edges have strictly
positive lengths $\{L_s\}$, and let $D$ be an observed dissimilarity map. If
$\lVert D-D_T\rVert_\infty < \tfrac12\min_s L_s$, then neighbor-joining applied to $D$ returns $T$.
\end{theorem}
For the theorem's fixed true metric, the bottleneck ratio
$m=\min_s L_s/(2\lVert D-D_T\rVert_\infty)>1$ is a sufficient certificate of exact recovery. It is
conservative, so NJ can recover the topology when $m\leq1$. In our experiments the true topology is
known but its edge lengths are not; we fit nonnegative edge lengths on that topology to obtain
$D_{\mathrm{fit}}$ and report the analogous diagnostic
$\widetilde m=\min_s\widehat L_s/(2\lVert D-D_{\mathrm{fit}}\rVert_\infty)$. Because
$D_{\mathrm{fit}}$ is estimated from $D$ and some truth trees contain polytomies, $\widetilde m$ is a
theorem-motivated, label-aware diagnostic rather than a literal Atteson certificate. We therefore also
adopt a label-free proxy computed from $D$ alone, based on the classical four-point condition for tree
metrics~\citep{sempleSteel2003phylogenetics}.

\textit{Four-point additivity} ($A$). Non-additivity of a distance matrix is measured by the quartet
$\delta$-score of \citet{holland2002delta}. For each quartet of leaves the three pairwise-distance sums,
sorted $s_1\ge s_2\ge s_3$, give
\[
  \delta_q=\frac{s_1-s_2}{s_1-s_3}\in[0,1] ,
\]
which is $0$ exactly when the four-point condition $s_1=s_2$ holds \citep{sempleSteel2003phylogenetics}
(the additive, tree-metric case) and $1$ at the maximally non-treelike quartet (the two \emph{smallest}
sums equal, the largest strictly greater). We report its complement, averaged over the $Q$ quartets,
\[
  A=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\bigl(1-\delta_q\bigr)
   =\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\frac{s_2^{(q)}-s_3^{(q)}}{s_1^{(q)}-s_3^{(q)}}\in[0,1] ,
\]
so that $A=1$ is perfectly additive and larger $A$ tracks recovery (matching the direction of the
Atteson margin, whereas $\delta$ runs the other way). Because $A$ requires no labels,
it serves as our proxy when the true tree is unknown. For evaluation against a known tree we
additionally report $A$ over only the \emph{informative} quartets (those the truth resolves into two
cherries), since quartets that fall inside a polytomy carry no split to recover and only dilute the
all-quartet mean. We relate $A$ and the fitted Atteson diagnostic to recovery in \S\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
We structure our experiments around three questions: whether the model lineage or phylogeny can be
estimated from weight changes; whether those changes predict downstream held-out behavior; and
how behavior-based phylogeny differs from weight-based.

\paragraph{Controlled and real training trees.}
\label{sec:exp:trees}
% Base: Flan-T5-base (250M). Bernoulli branching, 4 tasks, 50 replications,
% 5--8 leaves, known topology by construction.
% 2000 steps, lr=3e-4, batch=8, grad_accum=4, bf16.
% Controlled design enables exact evaluation against the latent-ancestor leaf topology.

To create a controlled experiment, we generate stochastic model training trees beginning from a root model (process detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:details}), producing 50 trees with up to 10 leaf nodes. We randomly assign a task/dataset pair to each edge, and at each split fine-tune a child model from its parent on that pair. LoRA chains are sequentially merged so every evaluated leaf is a standalone full-weight checkpoint. We then estimate a phylogeny from the \textit{model leaves} and compare it to the known training tree, testing both direct lineage and phylogeny estimation.

We vary the root model, task types, and trained layers. Our primary topology evaluation uses an
encoder--decoder model (Flan-T5-base, 250M) and a decoder-only model (Llama-3.2-1B), fine-tuning on
Q\&A, classification, translation, and summarization. Auxiliary analyses use Pythia-160M for a
cross-architecture layer diagnostic and Llama-3.1-8B for a behavioral regression; we do not treat
these as an end-to-end scale sweep. For Flan-T5, we run full fine-tuning and four LoRA \citep{hu2022lora}
scopes (key only; query/key/value; query/key/value/output; all attention and feed-forward projections).
For Llama-3.2-1B, we run full fine-tuning and query/key/value LoRA at ranks 8 and 64. For each base
model, we use the same task instructions. We also
validate our method on three documented model lineages from HuggingFace. We highlight one in the
main text and report the other two in Appendix~\ref{app:organic-additional}.

\paragraph{Direct lineage.}
MoTHer~\citep{horwitz2025mother} assembles pairwise relatedness and direction scores into a directed
model tree, while Neural Phylogeny~\citep{yu2025neural} identifies parent--child relationships. Our
auxiliary ordering analysis instead works at the branch level among observed leaves. For each run we test whether same-branch leaf pairs are closer in weight space than cross-branch pairs with a one-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ test, summarize the effect with the rank-biserial correlation, and report the within-run branch correlation $r_{\mathrm{branch}}$ (Fisher $z$-averaged across runs)\footnote{For each run we compute $z=\operatorname{arctanh}(r)$, average on the $z$ scale, and back-transform with $r=\tanh(\bar z)$.} between weight distance and the same-branch indicator.

\paragraph{Phylogeny estimation.}
\label{sec:exp:weights}
Besides direct lineage, we test whether our method estimates the phylogeny. From \S\ref{sec:framework:theory}, accurate estimation should be possible when the distance matrix is sufficiently additive, for which the label-free four-point additivity $A$ is a practical proxy. For each tree we report RF, FN, clade recovery, and polytomy-aware exact recovery (PAER; $\mathrm{FN}=0$), relating them to the fitted Atteson diagnostic $\widetilde m$ and $A$ in \S\ref{sec:results}. The per-layer trees also let us identify which layers carry the most inherited signal, via the highest- and lowest-FN layers per architecture.


\paragraph{Predicting held-out behavior.}
\label{sec:exp:behavioral}
We next estimate how strongly whitebox weight distance predicts behavioral similarity on \textit{independent, held-out} probe tasks. We generate a new set of training trees in which translation is held out. For each prompt and model, we obtain three stochastic generations (temperature 1.0, top-$p$ 1.0) and embed each output with a small sentence-transformer (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}, $384$-d). We average paired cosine similarity over the complete prompt-by-draw grid, then report the random-effects DerSimonian--Laird \citep{dersimonianlaird1986} pooled correlation $r_{\mathrm{DL}}$ between weight distance and output-embedding similarity, with both quantities $z$-scored within run (placing all models and architectures on one scale). We also probe HellaSwag, ARC-C, MMLU, and TruthfulQA which to our knowledge neither Flan nor Llama trained on. Other generation details are reported in the reproducibility appendix and workbook.




\paragraph{Comparison to behavior-only estimation.}
\label{sec:exp:blackbox}
Sometimes weights are unavailable and provenance must be inferred from outputs alone; prior work estimates lineage from behavior in exactly this setting~\citep{wu2026llmdna, yax2025phylolm}. Because our documented HuggingFace family (\S\ref{sec:exp:trees}) has a known topology, we can score behavior-only estimation against its documented reference lineage rather than leaving it unsupervised. On that family we run the behavior-based PhyloLM method~\citep{yax2025phylolm}, using its released genes and genetic distance on the same checkpoints, and compare it against our weight-based reconstruction on the identical documented lineage (details in App.~\ref{app:blackbox}). Our controlled comparison runs PhyloLM on every tree in the fresh shared-tree Llama rank-8 LoRA, rank-64 LoRA, and full-FT conditions (50 reconstructions per condition). Topology metrics use the common 46-tree subset with at least one nontrivial unrooted truth split. The encoder--decoder Flan groups are outside PhyloLM's decoder-only scope.

\paragraph{Phylogeny estimation.}
\label{sec:results:weighttree}
Our methods also estimate the phylogeny, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:variants}, with respect to clade recovery, polytomy-aware exact recovery (PAER; $\mathrm{FN}=0$), and RF/FN for all eligible phylogenies. In the fresh shared-tree suite, clade recovery ranges from 87--98\% and PAER from 70--93\%. The merged LoRA variants recover 87--98\% of clades for Flan and 96--98\% for Llama; full fine-tuning recovers 95\% and 97\%, respectively. On the same 46 trees with a nontrivial truth split, weights recover 96\% versus PhyloLM's 35\% under rank-8 LoRA, 98\% versus 24\% at rank 64, and 97\% versus 52\% under full fine-tuning. We see in Appendix~\ref{app:withintask} that phylogeny estimation still holds when every node in a tree trains within a single task (clade recovery $59$--$87\%$); in that design, task-path distances are all zero and provide no discriminating baseline.

Treating layers as gene-like estimators reveals that query/key/value attention weights, and especially the key weights, can estimate the phylogeny as well as the all-layer aggregate in the full-fine-tuning regime. On the same 46 fresh shared-tree Flan runs, the encoder self-attention key subset recovers 95.5\% (SE 2.0\%) of clades, identical to the all-weight estimate; PAER is 89.1\% for both (App.~\ref{app:layers}). This subset contains 7.08M of 247.58M scalar weights (2.86\%, approximately 3\%). Layer importance differs by architecture (App.~\ref{app:layertrace}): signal is more concentrated in key weights for the encoder--decoder model and more diffuse across key weights in the decoder-only model.

Finally, Figure~\ref{fig:coherence_recovery} shows simulation-level, layer-subset, and individual-matrix recovery summaries from the same full-weight trees as Table~\ref{tab:variants}, together with the matched PhyloLM estimates for the three Llama conditions. Every point averages the same 46 topology-eligible runs rather than plotting runs as separate observations. Whole-weight simulation recovery ranges from 87--98\%, including 95\% for Flan full fine-tuning and 97\% for Llama full fine-tuning; post-hoc projection subsets recover 88--98\%. Individual q/k/v matrices retain substantial signal across both full fine-tuning and merged LoRA, with mean clade recovery spanning 71--99\%. The matched PhyloLM estimates recover 24--52\% of clades. Pooled fractional-logit fits show recovery rising with both the fitted Atteson diagnostic $\widetilde m$ and label-free additivity proxy $A$. Under a paired 2,000-replicate bootstrap that jointly resamples the 46 tree IDs, the recovery odds ratio is 5.77 (95\% bootstrap interval 3.18--12.18) per tenfold increase in mean $\widetilde m$, and 4.73 (3.29--7.14) per 0.10 increase in mean $A$. We treat these as empirical recovery diagnostics rather than guarantees.

\paragraph{Predicting held-out behavior.}
\label{sec:results:behavioral}

While estimating phylogeny from weights is useful for safety and provenance, it can be of even more use if similarity in weights indicates similarity in behavior. Inspired by \citet{cloud2025subliminal}'s subliminal-learning experiment, we test
whether models closer in weight space produce more similar outputs on held-out tasks, using translation
(on a tree that never trained on it) along with four probe tasks that, to our knowledge, neither model
was trained on (HellaSwag, ARC-Challenge, MMLU, TruthfulQA).
Table~\ref{tab:behavior_holdout} reports a consistent, significant negative random-effects correlation across all held-out
probes in Llama and for HellaSwag in Flan. Immediate EOS is an observed output and is represented as an
explicit empty-output semantic state in the primary analysis. In the Llama full-FT production runs,
30.03\% of HellaSwag outputs were empty, versus 0.10\% for TruthfulQA and less than 0.01\% for ARC-C
and MMLU. This choice is consequential for HellaSwag: including immediate EOS gives $r_{\mathrm{DL}}=-0.20$ [$-0.30,-0.10$], whereas excluding empty completions gives $-0.08$ [$-0.18,0.02$] and leaves the other probe estimates essentially unchanged (App.~\ref{app:empty-sensitivity}). classification-trained leaves collapse to their label vocabulary rather than translate, so similarity
reflects whether two leaves fail in the same way, not transmission of held-out translation behavior
(App.~\ref{app:translation_probe}).

\paragraph{Comparing to behavior-only lineage.}
\label{sec:results:blackbox}

When weights are unavailable or different architectures need to be compared, lineage must be inferred from behavior alone. We compare our weight-based
estimation to PhyloLM~\citep{yax2025phylolm}, scored against the
same documented lineage. On the real Mistral family (Fig.~\ref{fig:real-trees}), PhyloLM and weights
both recover all three forks. Their informative-quartet additivity differs ($A=0.84$ vs.\ $0.98$), but
we treat that as a distance-matrix diagnostic rather than evidence of better topology because both
reconstructions are exact.

The Mistral forks are large RLHF or direct-preference-optimization (DPO) weight changes that move behavior substantially. Our controlled trees are a more precise test. In the LoRA and low-update regimes fine-tuning barely shifts behavior, yet weight distance can still estimate phylogenies. Running PhyloLM on the fresh shared trees against the same ground truth, behavior-only recovery falls well below weight-based recovery: under full fine-tuning PhyloLM recovers $52\%$ of clades versus $97\%$ for weights, under rank-8 LoRA it recovers $35\%$ versus $96\%$, and under rank-64 LoRA it recovers $24\%$ versus $98\%$. Behavioral similarity is a real but attenuated and probe-dependent readout of weight distance ($|r_{\mathrm{DL}}|=0.08$--$0.33$ across interpretable held-out Llama probes), which helps explain why output-based distances can preserve some pairwise signal yet lose enough resolution to miss topology after small updates.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

We show that model family phylogeny can be estimated from weights alone, and that using the best layers---about 3\% of Flan's scalar weights in the encoder-key subset---estimates training trees as well as using all layers.  We find that clade recovery works across encoder--decoder and decoder-only models and survives LoRA training at low rank ($r=8$). Across our controlled conditions, PAER ($\mathrm{FN}=0$) ranges from 70--93\%. The fitted Atteson margin is moderately associated with recovery within architecture--training groups, whereas the label-free additivity proxy $A$ is only weakly associated; neither should be treated as a trust guarantee. In addition to estimating phylogeny and direct lineage from weights, we show that models closer in weight space tend to have more similar held-out outputs; the effect depends on the probe and ranges from $r_{\mathrm{DL}}=-0.33$ to $-0.08$ across interpretable held-out Llama probes. Finally, weight- and behavior-based estimation recover the same documented Mistral family, while weight-based estimation is substantially stronger on several controlled trees and requires no prompts.


We note that our experiments and methods have limitations.  One limitation is that our methods for phylogeny estimation using weights currently only work when the underlying architecture is identical, i.e., the weight space is the same.  This means that Llama cannot directly be compared to Mistral, for example. Another issue is that our controlled training tree generation results from a fairly simple process that may not result in the most realistic training trees.  We attempt to mitigate that with our real-world example. Future directions include better relating models across different architectures such as with CKA and further exploring the mechanism by which lineage is inherited. In summary, we hope that our work illuminates the need to consider models holistically and not as a series of benchmarks.

\section{Experimental Details}

\subsection*{Tree Generation}
\label{app:tree-gen}

Training trees are generated via a Poisson branching process as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The root node (pretrained Flan-T5-base) draws $k \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda = 1.5)$ children, with a minimum of 1.
    \item Each subsequent node draws $k \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda = 1.5)$ children. Nodes that draw $k = 0$ naturally become leaves; no separate pruning step is needed.
    \item Breadth First Search expansion continues until either a node budget ($n_\mathrm{nodes} = 14$ training nodes, not counting the root) is exhausted or a maximum depth ($n_\mathrm{layers} = 4$) is reached. The budget cap creates the truncation; any node alive when the budget runs out becomes a leaf.
    \item Each non-root node is assigned a task and dataset sampled without replacement from 4 task types (summarization, classification, QA, translation) and 36 datasets (9 summarization, 10 classification, 8 QA, 9 translation). The topology (parent--child structure) is fixed by a topology seed; dataset assignments vary across the 50 replications.
    \item Recovery excludes trees with fewer than four leaves or no nontrivial truth split, because their unrooted topology contains no clade that can be meaningfully recovered.
\end{enumerate}

This procedure yields trees with 4--8 observed leaves across the 50 replications, with variable depth and branching structure. The ground-truth topology is fully known by construction, enabling exact RF evaluation.

\paragraph{Sample accounting.} Each controlled grid targets 50 generated training trees, while
recovery and direct ordering have different eligibility rules. The fresh Flan comparison clones the
same 50 topology IDs across all five training regimes. The 46 truths with at least one nontrivial
unrooted split enter recovery, and the same 26 enter
ordering in every row; the other 24 trees have no cross-root-branch comparison, so ordering is
undefined rather than missing. The fresh Llama rank-8, rank-64, and full-FT regimes reuse those same
50 topology IDs and task/data assignments; the same 46 enter recovery and the same 26 enter ordering.
The fresh PhyloLM comparison generates all 50 trees in each shared-tree Llama condition, but its
topology summaries use the common 46-tree subset after excluding four truths with no nontrivial
unrooted split (topology IDs 015, 020, 037, and 047); matched weight values in
Table~\ref{tab:variants} are computed on those same 46 trees.
The Flan per-layer profile in Fig.~\ref{fig:layertrace_grid} is rescored from the saved distance cubes
on the same 46 topology-eligible fresh full-fine-tuning trees. The Llama panel remains the earlier
full-fine-tuning archive with $n=47$.

\subsection*{Training Configuration}
\paragraph{Flan-T5-base.} Each node trained for 2,000 steps, learning rate $3 \times 10^{-4}$, batch size 8, gradient accumulation 4 (effective batch 32), warmup 200 steps, weight decay 0.01, and bf16 precision. We used up to 10,000 training examples and 1,000 evaluation examples per dataset, with a fixed dataset seed across all nodes to avoid data variation as a confound.

\paragraph{Llama-3.2-1B.} We used the base model (not Instruct), pinned to revision \texttt{4e20de362430cd3b72f300e6b0f18e50e7166e08}, with completion-only causal loss, 2,000 maximum steps, batch size 1, gradient accumulation 32 (effective batch 32), 200 warmup steps, weight decay 0.01, bf16 precision, and maximum sequence length 640. Full fine-tuning used learning rate $2 \times 10^{-5}$; QKV LoRA used $3 \times 10^{-4}$, dropout 0.05, and $(r,\alpha)=(8,16)$ or $(64,128)$.

\section{Blackbox Experiment Details}
\label{app:blackbox}

\paragraph{PhyloLM reproduction.} For the behavior-only comparison on the documented Mistral family
(Fig.~\ref{fig:real-trees}), we reproduce PhyloLM \citep{yax2025phylolm} faithfully using its released
\texttt{math} gene bank and its published Nei-similarity genetic distance (upstream commit
\texttt{8c70edf}). We sample $G=128$ genes and, for each, draw $N=32$ raw completions of $4$ tokens per
model (temperature $1$, no chat template, matching PhyloLM's design), take the $4$-character allele,
form the per-gene allele populations, compute the Nei similarity between every pair of the ten
checkpoints, convert to a distance ($-\log$ of the similarity), and build the tree by neighbor-joining.
The reconstruction is scored against the same documented truth tree and with the same polytomy-aware
scorer used for the whitebox families.

The fresh controlled comparison uses the same locked PhyloLM contract ($G=128$, $N=32$, four-token
continuations, temperature $1$) with sampling seed \texttt{20260803} on the 50 shared-tree Llama
rank-8 LoRA, rank-64 LoRA, and full-FT conditions. For LoRA leaves, we reconstruct and query the
ordered cumulative root-to-leaf adapter chain; full-FT leaves are loaded directly. Each condition
contains 50 completed reconstructions and 365 leaf populations, and all generated alleles are
nonempty. The four trees whose truths contain no nontrivial unrooted split are valid behavioral
reconstructions but cannot define clade recovery, PAER, RF, or FN, leaving the same $n=46$ topology
sample in every fresh row of Table~\ref{tab:variants}.

\section{Additional Organic Lineage Checks}
\label{app:organic-additional}

The paper evaluates three documented organic HuggingFace lineages in total. The ten-checkpoint
Mistral-7B family is the main-text example because it contains three documented nontrivial splits and
supports the matched PhyloLM comparison. Two smaller checks establish that its whitebox result is not
unique to Mistral. In a seven-checkpoint Flan-T5-base lineage, averaged-matrix NJ and the BHV Fr\'echet
mean recover the sole documented Flan-Alpaca descendant clade (FN $=0/1$); its complementary split has
98.2\% Fr\'echet support under cosine distance. In a ten-checkpoint Llama-2-7B lineage, the cosine
Fr\'echet estimate likewise recovers the sole documented seven-model chat-descendant clade (FN
$=0/1$), with 95.2\% support for the complementary base/Nous-Hermes/Vicuna split. These two references
are bushy and specify no ordering within the recovered clade, so we claim recovery of one documented
split in each, not complete resolution of their descendant histories.


\section{Behavioral Regression: Full Results}
\label{app:regression}

All models regress behavioral output similarity (sentence-embedding cosine, translation probe
unless noted) on cosine weight distance with run fixed effects and SEs clustered on run. We report
three coefficients per condition (Table~\ref{tab:regression_betas}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Raw $\beta$}: the unscaled slope. Negative in every audited condition, although
    statistical support varies by condition. It is
    \emph{not} comparable across conditions: LoRA groups have far smaller absolute cosine distances,
    so their raw $\beta$ is inflated by orders of magnitude (e.g.\ $-56$ for LoRA over attention+FFN
    projections vs.\ $-0.20$ for key-only LoRA) without implying a larger effect.
  \item \textbf{Semi-standardized $\beta$}: predictor $z$-scored within run; reads as ``$\Delta$
    output similarity per 1 within-run SD of weight distance.'' Interpretable within a condition,
    but still not cross-comparable because conditions differ in how much their \emph{outputs} vary
    (k\_only translations are near-identical, so its semi-standardized $\beta$ is tiny despite the
    strongest correlation).
  \item \textbf{Standardized $\beta$ (within-run)}: both sides $z$-scored within run. This is the
    only cross-condition-comparable effect size, and it equals the within-run correlation. It is the
    number reported in the main text.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pooling estimator.} Because each run has its own tree and hence its own weight-space scale, a
raw pooled slope is not meaningful across runs; we therefore standardize within run (equivalently, treat
the run as a blocking factor), at which point the slope equals the within-run correlation. To pool these
per-run correlations we use a random-effects DerSimonian--Laird meta-analysis of the per-run Fisher-$z$
values rather than an equal-weight mean, since runs vary in leaf count and noise. As an estimator-robustness check on a representative full-FT
condition (Llama-3.2-1B, translation probe, centroid DV), the pooling choices agree in sign and
magnitude (random-effects $r_{\mathrm{DL}}=-0.161$ ($95\%$ CI $[-0.28,-0.04]$, $p=0.011$), equal-weight mean
$r=-0.21$, one-stage mixed model with a random slope $r=-0.136$), with genuine between-run heterogeneity
($I^2\approx54\%$) that is not explained by leaf count. We report $r_{\mathrm{DL}}$; the within-run partial
associations underlying it are shown per condition in Fig.~\ref{fig:regression_diagnostics}.

\section{Translation Probe Outputs for TaskHoldout Models}
\label{app:translation_probe}

The \texttt{runs\_no\_translation\_v2} condition trains models on summarization,
QA, and classification only, with translation withheld from the task pool. We use
the translation probe to test whether weight distance predicts behavioral similarity
even when the probe task was never trained. However, this condition reveals a
fundamental limitation of behavioral probing on out-of-distribution tasks: models
whose most recent fine-tuning was on classification have overwritten the base
model's translation ability entirely, producing degenerate single-token outputs
rather than translations.

classification output only the label vocabulary of their fine-tuning task,
regardless of input.

Because classification-trained nodes produce degenerate outputs, pairwise semantic
similarity between nodes is driven by whether each model can translate at all,
not by genealogical proximity. We therefore retain the $r_{\mathrm{DL}}=+0.11$
[$+0.02,+0.21$] cell as a \emph{degeneration diagnostic}, not as evidence that the

(translating vs.\ emitting a trained-task label) rather than held-out translation behavior.

This degenerate-output collapse is specific to the encoder--decoder Flan-T5 TaskHoldout condition,
where classification-trained nodes overwrite translation entirely. It is \emph{not} a general property
of held-out probes. In the matched full-fine-tuning held-out conditions of
Table~\ref{tab:behavior_holdout} the nodes remain capable on the held-out task, and there the
weight$\to$behavior bridge is interpretable for Llama-3.2-1B: it is negative and strengthens when
translation is withheld. The weakly positive Flan-T5 result remains the degeneration diagnostic
described here rather than a clean reversal of the bridge.

\section{Task-Metadata Baseline}
\label{app:taskbaseline}

To test whether weight-based recovery merely reflects task identity, we build a baseline that uses
\emph{only} task labels and no weights, and run it through the same neighbor-joining pipeline. For each
leaf we read its root-to-leaf sequence of task types from the manifest (e.g.\ summarization $\to$
summarization $\to$ translation). The metadata distance between two leaves is the length of the two
task-type sequences not covered by their shared prefix, $d(i,j)=(\lvert s_i\rvert-p)+(\lvert
s_j\rvert-p)$ with $p$ the shared-prefix length, an ancestry analog in which leaves sharing a longer
initial training-task sequence are placed closer. We apply neighbor-joining to this distance and score
the reconstruction against the same ground-truth trees with the same polytomy-aware metric as the
weight reconstructions.

Many leaves share identical task paths, so this metadata distance contains extensive ties and its NJ
reconstruction depends on tie handling. We therefore use it only as a qualitative diagnostic and do
not report a quantitative task-path benchmark. The within-task design below provides the stricter
task-control: every task-path distance is exactly zero, so task metadata has no discriminating power.

\paragraph{Verifying the organic reference lineages.} For the real HuggingFace families
(\S\ref{sec:results:weighttree}), the documented reference tree is taken from the checkpoints' model cards and
then corroborated against each repository's \texttt{config.json}: the declared base model, the tokenizer
vocabulary size, and the hidden size / layer count. For the Mistral-7B-v0.1 family this confirms all ten
checkpoints share the architecture ($4096$ hidden, $32$ layers) and separates the two vocabulary groups
($32000$ vs.\ $32002$, the latter adding control tokens), consistent with the documented fork structure.
We treat the reference as a documented-and-verified lineage, not as unverified metadata.


\section{Within-Task Recovery}
\label{app:withintask}
